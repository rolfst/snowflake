# Essay Writing Skill

## When to Use

Load this skill when:
- Writing a new essay or article from scratch
- Rewriting or editing an existing essay
- The user asks to apply "Orwell rules" or "essay style"
- The output is public-facing prose (Substack, LinkedIn, party publication)

Do NOT load for: policy plans, technical documentation, code comments, management summaries, or internal planning documents.

---

## The Seven Rules

These rules apply to every sentence. Work through the draft once per rule, or apply all seven in a single pass for short pieces.

**Rule 1: Never use a metaphor, simile, or figure of speech you have seen in print.**
Fresh comparison or none. "Level playing field," "going forward," "at the end of the day" are dead. Cut them or replace them with something concrete.

**Rule 2: Never use a long word where a short one will do.**
"Utilise" is "use." "Facilitate" is "help." "Commence" is "start." "Subsequent" is "next." If the shorter word carries the same meaning, use it.

**Rule 3: If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out.**
Read every sentence and ask: does each word earn its place? "The reason why" is "why." "In order to" is "to." "At this point in time" is "now."

**Rule 4: Never use the passive where you can use the active.**
"Mistakes were made" becomes "we made mistakes." "The proposal was rejected" becomes "they rejected the proposal." The active voice names the actor.

**Rule 5: Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word, or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday equivalent.**
"Ex ante" is "in advance." "Paradigm shift" is "change." "Leverage" (as a verb) is "use." When technical terms are necessary, define them immediately in plain language.

**Rule 6: Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.**
Rules serve the writing. If following a rule produces an ugly or misleading sentence, the rule loses.

**Rule 7: If you can cut a sentence out with a small rewrite, cut it.**
This is the rule that removes AI-sounding text. A sentence that only restates what the previous sentence already said does not earn its place. Rewrite the first sentence to carry both meanings, or cut the second. Do not keep both.

## Register: Rolfst Essay Style

These are additional constraints specific to Rolf's published work, derived from `who-owns-tomorrow`, `two-bugs-one-crash`, and `radical-honesty-EN`.

- **No em-dashes, no double-dashes.** Use commas, colons, or full stops instead. Parenthetical additions go in parentheses or get rewritten into the sentence.
- **No campaign language.** "We believe in," "together we build," "a better Europe" are forbidden. State the argument, not the aspiration.
- **No staccato closing sentences in essay sections.** Short punchy sentences work in management documents. In essays, the sentence carries the argument. A one-line paragraph that just confirms what the previous paragraph argued is not an ending. Rewrite it into the paragraph or cut it.
- **Concrete before abstract.** If you are making an abstract claim, anchor it first with a number, a name, or a specific event. "Housing costs rose" is weaker than "Dutch household energy bills hit 3,000 to 4,000 euros a year during the 2022 gas crisis."
- **Use contractions.** "Didn't," "won't," "it's," "they're" read more naturally than their full forms in essay prose. The full form ("did not," "will not") is appropriate for emphasis only: "He did not merely adjust the plan. He rewrote it entirely." If there is no emphasis intended, contract.
- **No author bio for internal pieces.** The bio line ("Rolf Strijdhorst is co-founder of...") is for public publication only. Remove it for internal documents, forum posts, and party communications.

## Essay vs. Management Document

| Feature | Essay | Management document |
|---|---|---|
| Short punchy closing sentence | Avoid | Use |
| Bullet point lists | Use sparingly | Use freely |
| Section summaries | Cut | Keep |
| Abstract opening | Cut | Acceptable |
| Numbered rules / steps | Use only when listing is the point | Default format |

## Workflow

When rewriting an existing piece:

1. Read the full piece once without editing.
2. Apply Rule 7 first: find every sentence that only restates the previous one. Rewrite or cut.
3. Apply Rules 1-5 in a single pass: flag dead metaphors, long words, passive constructions, jargon.
4. Check register rules: em-dashes, campaign language, staccato closings.
5. Read aloud (mentally). If a sentence causes you to stumble, rewrite it.
6. Word count check: a well-edited essay is typically 15-25% shorter than the first draft without losing substance.

When writing from a plan:

1. Write the full draft following the plan's content outline.
2. Do not self-edit while drafting. Finish the full draft first.
3. Then apply the workflow above.

## Examples

**Before (Rule 7 violation -- restatement):**
> None of those three is fixed. All three respond to effort.

**After:**
> All three respond to effort.

---

**Before (Rule 7 violation -- implicit contrast that a conjunction can carry):**
> They did not cut the plan to make it easier to pass. They built the support to pass the plan as it was.

**After:**
> They did not cut the plan to make it easier to pass, but built the support to pass it as it was.

**Pattern to watch for:** Two consecutive sentences where the second is the logical counterpart of the first (contrast, consequence, or elaboration). If a single conjunction ("but," "and," "while," "so") can join them without losing meaning, join them.

**Exception -- deliberate emphasis:** A short sentence that lands the point of a paragraph is not a staccato closing. The difference is whether the short sentence is the destination of the paragraph or merely a punchier restatement of what the paragraph already said. "The proposal doesn't tax success. It taxes extraction." works because the whole paragraph builds toward that distinction -- the short sentence is where the argument arrives, not where it echoes. A staccato closing, by contrast, restates the paragraph's conclusion in fewer words after the conclusion has already been made. The test: remove the short sentence. If the paragraph's argument weakens, the sentence is doing real work and belongs there. If the paragraph is unchanged, cut it or fold it in.

---

**Before (staccato closing):**
> Parties that cannot be seen lose elections too. They just lose them quietly.

**After:**
> A movement that cannot be seen loses elections as surely as one that swings too far, only without the noise to show it tried.

---

**Before (passive + jargon):**
> The utilisation of coordinated fiscal mechanisms has been proposed as a solution.

**After:**
> Coordinated tax policy can solve this.

## Nederlandse essays

Dezelfde zeven regels gelden voor Nederlandse teksten. De toepassing verschilt op een aantal punten.

**Regel 1: Geen dode metaforen.**
Nederlandse clichés om te vermijden: "in het licht van," "een stap in de goede richting," "het speelveld gelijktrekken," "de handschoen oppakken," "aan de slag gaan met."

**Regel 2: Kort woord boven lang woord.**
"Gebruikmaken van" is "gebruiken." "Bewerkstelligen" is "bereiken." "Thematiseren" is "benoemen." "Verduidelijken" is "uitleggen." "Faciliteren" is "mogelijk maken."

**Regel 3: Schrap woorden die niets toevoegen.**
"Ten behoeve van" is "voor." "In het kader van" is "bij" of weg. "Teneinde" is "om." "Met betrekking tot" is "over." "Op het gebied van" is "in" of weg.

**Regel 4: Actief boven passief.**
Nederlands verbergt het passief makkelijker dan Engels door onpersoonlijke constructies: "er wordt gesteld dat" wordt "hij stelt dat." "Er is besloten om" wordt "zij besloten." Noem de handelende persoon.

**Regel 5: Geen jargon zonder uitleg.**
Zelfde als Engels. Economisch en beleidsmatig jargon direct in gewone taal uitleggen.

**Regel 6: Breek een regel liever dan lelijk te schrijven.**
Zelfde.

**Regel 7: Schrap zinnen die met een kleine herschrijving in één zin passen.**
Zelfde. Nederlandse bijzinnen verleiden tot stapelen waar het omgekeerde beter werkt: lange bijzinconstructies eerder opsplitsen dan samenvoegen.

### Aanvullende regels voor Nederlands

- **Geen em-dashes, geen dubbele koppeltekens.** Gebruik komma's, dubbele punten of punten.
- **Geen campagnetaal.** "Wij geloven in," "samen bouwen we," "een beter Europa" zijn verboden. Formuleer het argument, niet de ambitie.
- **Opsommingen eindigen altijd op "en," nooit op een komma.** "Rood, wit en blauw" -- niet "rood, wit, blauw." De laatste twee elementen in een reeks worden verbonden door "en," niet gescheiden door een komma. Dit geldt ook voor langere reeksen: "belasting, inkomen, vermogen en arbeid" -- niet "belasting, inkomen, vermogen, arbeid."
- **Vermijd de ambtelijke omslachtige constructie** in plaats van de contractieregel. In het Nederlands gebruik je zelden apostrof-contracties in essays, maar de volle ambtelijke vorm verlengt zinnen onnodig. "Hij heeft geen wetgeving aangenomen" is beter dan "Er is door hem geen wetgeving aangenomen."
- **Geen AI-slogannen.** Verboden: "cruciaal," "robuust," "faciliteren," "inzetten op," "het is belangrijk om op te merken," "navigeren," "stroomlijnen," "versterken" (als holle werkwoord).

### Nederlandse voorbeelden

**Voor (Regel 7 -- herhaling):**
> Geen van de drie ligt vast. Alle drie reageren op inspanning.

**Na:**
> Alle drie reageren op inspanning.

---

**Voor (Regel 7 -- impliciete tegenstelling die een voegwoord kan dragen):**
> Ze sneden het plan niet bij om het makkelijker door te krijgen. Ze bouwden de steun op om het door te krijgen zoals het was.

**Na:**
> Ze sneden het plan niet bij om het makkelijker door te krijgen, maar bouwden de steun op om het door te krijgen zoals het was.

---

**Voor (opsomming zonder "en"):**
> Belasting, inkomen, vermogen, arbeid bepalen de uitkomst.

**Na:**
> Belasting, inkomen, vermogen en arbeid bepalen de uitkomst.

---

**Voor (passief + ambtelijk):**
> Er wordt door de beweging gesteld dat hervormingen noodzakelijk zijn.

**Na:**
> De beweging stelt dat hervormingen nodig zijn.
